## Tenths
Extending the pattern of hours, minutes, and seconds, seconds should be subdivided into 60 thirds. Instead, we subdivide them into 1000 milliseconds. Imagine how living in a world with thirds would feel for a moment. Movies would run at 0.4 fpt (frames per third), fluorescent lights would flicker at 1.67 or 2 fpt, and games would strive for 1 fpt.

In the world we actually live in, decimalization and metrication became symbols of reason and revolution in 1790s France and America. Decimal time was officially enacted in France in 1793. Each day would be divided into 10 hours. Each hour would be subdivided into 100 minutes. Each minute would be subdivided into 100 seconds. The resulting 100,000 seconds per day is close enough to our 86,400 seconds per day that decimal seconds feel surprisingly reasonable. Decimal hours and minutes did not. The revolutionary regime gave up on enforcing this unpopular change in 1795. (Swatch would resurrect decimal time 200 years later as Swatch Internet Time. This was also not widely adopted.)

Unlike decimal seconds, decimal meters and decimal grams (as well as decimal cents!) did successfully spread. By the mid-1800s, there was a clear consensus that any subdivision of seconds should be decimal, like the other metric units. *Tenths* was a long established English word for fractions. English *ten* (and its inverse *tenth*) shares an origin with German *zehn* or Dutch *tien*, rather than French *dix*, Spanish *diez*, or Latin *decem*. Preserving the original term was not a foregone conclusion. For example, English generally prefers the Latin word *percent* (short for *per centum*, meaning "by the hundred") and derived terms *percentage* and *percentile* over its native *hundredth* (German *hundert*, Dutch *honderd*).

California tycoon Leland Stanford (who founded Stanford in 1885) commissioned an 1878 photo series, *The Horse in Motion*. Automatic electrophotographs captured how horses actually moved, to a degree that humans had never been able to achieve even in centuries of close observation. The commonly depicted "flying gallop" pose, airborne with all four legs outstretched, was never actually a part of a running horse's gait. The indifferent, objective machine proved that what we thought we saw with our eyes was wrong. The photos were an international sensation, warranting a cover story in Scientific American.

In 1823, astronomer Friedrich Bessel observed consistent errors while recording very precise event timings. He created the concept of the "personal equation" to explain the errors: different people had different, but predictable, reaction times. There's a lot of individual variation, but it takes most people 0.2 to 0.3 seconds to notice a stimulus and perform an action in response. This raised a philosophical dilemma. What was the point of an imperfect human in the age of machines?

The shift to impressionist and pointillist styles of painting involved a similar reaction to the spread of photography. If a camera could capture a landscape or stern expression more perfectly than any human could hope to, what was the point of competing directly against the machine at capturing reality?

Official times at the 1896 Olympics were kept by individual referees with stopwatchs. This was just enough precision to keep records to the tenth of a second. People tried many different things in the following decades to achieve hundredths of a second-level Olympic record accuracy, but kept running up against the limits of human reaction time. Any precision beyond tenths would require that humans be removed from the loop entirely.
