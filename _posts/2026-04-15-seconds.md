## Seconds
In 1707, four British warships miscalculated their position and ran aground off the Isles of Scilly, killing 2,000 sailors in what is still one of the worst maritime disasters in British history. For lack of a better approach, the way ships calculated their longitude at that time was dead reckoning, basically guessing how far they'd gone from the last known port. The disaster prompted the Longitude Act of 1714, establishing a £20,000 (over £4M adjusted for inflation) bounty for the deployment of a reliable method to determine a ship's longitude within 30 minutes (half of a degree).

This is relevant to our history because one way to determine longitude in theory is to precisely measure the sun's position and compare it to solar noon at a known location, like the Greenwich Royal Observatory. If you know the exact time, you can calculate your exact longitude from that single observation. The problem was, no one had a way to know the exact time at sea. Our marvelous pendulum clocks wouldn't work on the open ocean due to the motion of the waves.

The problem lit a fire in the imagination of English clockmaker John Harrison, who presented his first prototype for a marine chronometer to the Royal Academy in 1730. A lifetime of iteration and experimentation resulted in his fourth attempt finally winning the prize in 1761. In its maiden test, the H4 chronometer drifted just 5 seconds over the course of the 81-day voyage, enabling a longitude measurement accurate within 1.25 minutes.

As alluded to earlier, the word *second* comes from classical Latin *secunda*, in particular its used in the Latin phrase _pars minūta secunda_, meaning “second very small part”. We could have called minutes "primes" or "firsts" instead to maintain internal consistency, but that is not what we did.

The delicacy and expense involved in the construction of a device so precise made this solution was barely affordable for even the richest country in the world, with one chronometer costing about one-third the price of an entire ship. Ordinary people wouldn't have access to clocks precise to the second until replaceable parts and the growth of manufacturing made them affordable in the mid-1800s.

The metric system, as first adopted by French revolutionaries in 1799, only included base units of distance and mass (meters and kilograms), not time. Carl Gauss proposed using the second, with a new official definition of 1/86,400 of a day, as the standard metric unit of time in his millimeter/milligram/second measurement system in 1832. This proposal gained wide acceptance by 1862. The second was formally adopted as an official metric unit in the centimeter/gram/second measurement system, proposed by a committee that included James Maxwell, Lord Kelvin, and James Joule, in 1874.

Miniaturization followed mass production and resulted in the construction of pocket watches. Widely available second precision enabled new levels of coordination that would have been unreliable just decades earlier. Military watches were first issued to officers in the 1880s. At the beginning of a battle, everyone would synchronize their watches to keep their maneuvers coordinated. In particular, it was important to agree on the exact second to expect an artillery strike. "Synchronize your watches" is an artifact of the second era, where you might not know the absolute time, but you could act like you did if you kept everyone's relative times the same.

But if you did in fact need to know the absolute time, observatories began broadcasting official time signals by telegraph in the 1850s. It wasn't a service the average person would have access to. That begins with the advent of radio time stations in 1924. Time hotlines you could telephone for an accurate time signal started appearing in 1933 and gradually displaced radio time stations.

Wristwatches, then called "bracelet watches", were originally a women's fashion accessory, as the name implies. They only lost their genderedness after military wristwatches entered common use around the world in the 1880s and 1890s. Newly verified as manly, men's wristwatches became a standard fashion accessory in the 1900s. They remain the most popular men's jewelry item to this day.
