Fly a series of sorties against impossible odds as an ace fighter pilot with a team of wingmen. It's a cliched scenario that had been done in a hundred games. Even in three dimensions, as early as Star Wars (1983). But to match the cliche with another, it had never been done as well as this.

Star Fox 64 came bundled with a Rumble Pak, plugging into the N64 controller's expansion slot to provide force feedback for a console game for the first time. Despite its three-pronged awkwardness, that controller fit your grip like an inverse glove. It's more apparent how natural it felt if you compare it to the PSX and Saturn controllers. While your wingmen embodied broad, conveniently animal-coded archetypes, they were distinctive, bantered with you as you rescued each other, and were *fully voiced*. All of that combined to create the most immersive experience available on a home console.

Star Fox 64 wasn't just an arcade-quality immersive experience you could play at home. You could play through it once in two hours to see a complete story. Or you could dig a little deeper and find three different campaigns that hidden sub-goals moved you between. Every level in each of those campaigns also had strict mdeal scoring thresholds that rewarded mastery. It was a calculated echo of the score attacks that defined 1980s shooters.

Nintendo's Kyoto EAD division created Star Fox 64, including legendary producer Shigeru Miyamoto and legendary composer Koji Kondo. Both creators also worked on Star Fox (1993) and would go on to make Ocarina of Time (1998).

One interesting thing Star Fox 64 did was retell its predecessor's story. It covered the same story beats Star Fox did, including detail that was infeasible just four years earlier. Nintendo had actually done the same thing already, with EarthBound (1994) telling the same story as Mother (1989). And the Switch 2 Star Fox (2026) plans to do it again, fleshing out Fox's origin story with yet more detail and improved gameplay.

Star Fox 64 fit into the genre of rail shooter. That described games like shoot-em-ups, but where you travel a set path into the screen, as opposed to right or up. Some early genre influences include Tempest (1981), Space Harrier (1985), and Panzer Dragoon (1995). It lost favor after the 1990s, when technological advancements made it cheaper to offer free movement in 3D space. This century, rail shooters are mostly limited to genre mashups such as Rez (2001), Child of Eden (2011), and New Pokémon Snap (2021).

You could describe 1997 as video game series figuring out how they worked in 3D, now that Super Mario 64 (1996) had shown it could be done masterfully. Final Fantasy VII (1997) made the transition masterfully, while GoldenEye 007 (1997) drew in console gamers with a taste of Quake (1996) LAN parties. Contemporary reviews panned Castlevania: Symphony of the Night (1997) for sticking with old, tired 2D. Sony even declined to market it, concerned it looked old-fashioned for the PSX. We only consider it the best-looking game that year in retrospect.
